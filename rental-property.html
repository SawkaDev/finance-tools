Should you buy this rental?

A rental property is four investments wearing one coat: cash flow, loan paydown, appreciation and tax shelter. Most calculators price the first and wave at the rest. This one builds the entire year-by-year pro forma, runs depreciation and recapture through the actual passive-loss rules, taxes the sale properly, stress-tests the deal until it breaks — and then asks the question almost no listing wants asked: would an index fund have beaten it?

What is actually computed. For each year of the hold the model builds a complete operating statement: gross scheduled rent grown at your rent growth, less vacancy and credit loss, plus other income, giving effective gross income. Nine expense lines are grown at three separate inflation rates — general expenses, property tax and insurance move independently because in the real world they do. Management is charged on collected income, not scheduled rent. Turnover cost is leasing fee plus make-ready, charged at your turnover frequency. Subtracting all of it gives NOI; subtracting debt service gives cash flow before tax.

Debt is amortised monthly, not annually. A twelve-month inner loop splits every payment into interest and principal so the interest deduction and the loan balance are both exact. An interest-only period is supported and the loan is re-amortised over the remaining term when it ends. Setting the down payment to 100% removes the loan entirely.

Depreciation is the part most calculators get wrong. Basis is purchase price less land, plus capitalised closing costs, plus rehab — not the price you paid. Year one uses the mid-month convention, so a December closing gets 1.5/12 of a year, not a full one. Loan points are amortised over the loan term rather than deducted, with the unamortised remainder written off at payoff. CapEx reserves are treated the way the IRS treats them: as capital, added to basis and depreciated from the year they are spent, not as a current deduction — which is why your taxable income is higher than your cash flow suggests. Cost segregation reclassifies your chosen share of basis and expenses it immediately under the 100% bonus depreciation that OBBBA made permanent for property acquired after 19 January 2025.

Losses do not simply vanish, and they do not simply deduct. Under §469 a rental loss is passive. With active participation the model applies the $25,000 special allowance and phases it out at 50 cents per dollar of MAGI above $100,000, reaching zero at $150,000. Whatever cannot be used is suspended and carried forward, offsets future passive income year by year, and is released in full against the gain in the year you sell. Choosing real-estate-professional status or the short-term-rental route (average stay of seven days or less, with material participation) removes the ceiling. The §199A deduction is applied only to positive net rental income, never to losses.

The sale is taxed in the right order. Net proceeds are sale price less selling costs less loan payoff. Gain is measured against adjusted basis — original basis plus capitalised CapEx minus all accumulated depreciation, so depreciation you took reduces basis and enlarges the gain. That gain is then split: any cost-segregation and bonus depreciation is recaptured first at ordinary rates under §1245; straight-line depreciation is recaptured as unrecaptured §1250 gain at the lesser of 25% or your ordinary rate; only what remains is taxed at the long-term capital gains rate. NIIT adds 3.8% on top when ticked. Suspended passive losses are released against all of it. Electing a 1031 exchange defers the entire bill and the tool reports the size of what you deferred, because a deferral is a liability you still own.

Why the index comparison is built the way it is. Comparing a leveraged property IRR against an unleveraged index return is not a comparison, it is a category error — leverage raises expected return and raises risk together. The honest version holds the cash flows identical: the index path starts with your full out-of-pocket at closing, and every year the property demands more money, the index receives the same amount. Positive property cash flow is reinvested at the index return in a side account. Both terminal values pay capital gains on their gains. What is left is a difference in dollars you could actually spend.

Rules of thumb, and what they are worth. The 1% rule (monthly rent ≥ 1% of price) and the 50% rule (operating expenses ≈ 50% of gross income) are screening filters from a lower-rate era; at 7%+ mortgage rates very few markets clear 1%, and the scorecard reports both against the actual model rather than instead of it. The 50% rule is checked against your own expense build-up, so a large divergence is a signal that one of your two estimates is wrong. Negative leverage — cap rate below the loan constant — is flagged explicitly, because it is the condition under which borrowing more makes your return worse rather than better, and it is the defining feature of most 2026 deals.

Benchmarks used in the scorecard. DSCR of 1.25 is the common lender floor, with 1.0 the absolute minimum on a DSCR product. Cash-on-cash of 8%+ is conventionally "strong", 4–8% acceptable. Vacancy of 5% represents one vacant month in twenty. CapEx reserves of 5–15% of rent, management at 8–10% for single-family and 4–6% for larger multifamily, landlord insurance 15–25% above a homeowners policy. Break-even occupancy above 85% is treated as fragile because a single bad tenant erases the year.

Known simplifications, stated plainly. One tax rate is applied to ordinary income rather than a full bracket walk, so a loss large enough to drop you a bracket is valued slightly too highly. The $25,000 allowance is not halved for married-filing-separately. State-level differences in depreciation conformity, transfer tax and rent control are not modelled. Refinancing, HELOCs, portfolio-level financing and partnership splits are out of scope. Cost-segregated components are all recaptured as §1245 ordinary income, which slightly overstates recapture on the 15-year land-improvement slice. The index alternative pays capital gains only at the end, ignoring dividend drag along the way. Insurance availability — not price, availability — is the single largest unmodelled risk in coastal and wildfire markets.

The one thing this cannot know. The property. No model can price a foundation crack, a tenant who stops paying, a neighbourhood that turns, or an insurer that leaves the state. The inputs above are estimates you supplied, and the stress panel exists because the honest use of a model like this is to find out how wrong you can be and still be fine.
